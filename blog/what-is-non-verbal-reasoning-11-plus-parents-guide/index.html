What Is Non Verbal Reasoning? A Parent's Guide

10 November 2026

Non-verbal reasoning is a test of pattern, shape and spatial logic: your child looks at sequences of figures and works out the rule that connects them. It appears in the 11+ for grammar and selective schools, including GL Assessment papers used across Barnet, Sutton and other selective areas, and it needs no vocabulary or maths beyond basic shapes.

What is non-verbal reasoning and what skills does it test?

Non-verbal reasoning (NVR) is a test of how well a child can spot patterns, rotate shapes and follow visual logic, all without reading a single word. Questions use shapes, codes and sequences instead of text, which is why schools like it: it measures reasoning that is harder to coach through vocabulary alone. The core skills are:

  • Pattern spotting: what changes from one shape to the next.
  • Rotation and reflection: mentally turning 2D and 3D figures.
  • Spatial awareness: how shapes fit together or fold.
  • Rule-based logic: cracking a code by working out the rule it follows.

GL Assessment, the board behind many grammar school 11+ papers, groups these into question types such as similarities, sequences, codes and odd-one-out in its practice materials. If your child can explain a rule in pictures, they can do NVR.

What is the difference between verbal and non-verbal reasoning?

Verbal reasoning tests words: vocabulary, synonyms, letter codes and logic written in sentences. Non-verbal reasoning tests pictures: shapes, patterns and spatial logic, with no reading required. Both appear in GL Assessment 11+ papers for grammar schools in Barnet, Sutton and other selective areas.

| | Verbal reasoning | Non-verbal reasoning | |---|---|---| | What it tests | Vocabulary, word logic, codes | Pattern, shape and spatial logic | | Looks like | Word analogies, letter sequences | "Which shape comes next?", odd-one-out | | Skills that help | Wide reading, spelling | Puzzles, drawing, spotting patterns |

Why grammar schools test both

Schools want a rounded picture of ability. A child with a strong vocabulary but weaker visual logic, or the reverse, would score unevenly if only one were tested. Testing both also reduces the advantage of heavy tutoring on a single question style.

For parents, the practical split is this: verbal reasoning improves through reading; non-verbal reasoning improves through short, regular practice with shape puzzles. What non-verbal reasoning question types appear in GL Assessment 11+ papers?

GL Assessment non-verbal papers draw on a small, repeatable set of question families: sequences, analogies, odd one out, matrices, codes, and 3D spatial shapes such as cubes and nets. Knowing the families matters because each has a fixed solving routine a child can rehearse. Areas like Kent name the exam board and paper structure on their admissions pages (Kent school admissions), so you can match practice to the actual paper rather than guess.

Sequences and series

A row of shapes changes step by step; the child picks the next one. Rotation, shading and added elements are the usual moves.

Analogies and relationships

Shape A relates to shape B the way shape C relates to what? Look for the single transformation, then apply it.

Odd one out

Five or six similar figures, one breaks the shared rule. Name the rule out loud before choosing.

Matrices and grids

A 3x3 grid with one empty cell; patterns run across rows and down columns. Check both directions before answering.

Codes and symbols

Each symbol maps to a letter or number; the child decodes the rule from given examples, then applies it to a new figure.

Cubes, nets and 3D spatial questions

Folding nets, cube views from different sides, and hole-punch or rotation tasks. These are the hardest to practise on paper and benefit most from repeated timed drills.

What do non-verbal reasoning questions look like? (worked examples)

The three formats below cover most of what appears in GL Assessment-style papers. Each one shows the reasoning a child should say out loud, because describing the rule is the fastest way to spot whether they understood it or guessed.

Worked example: a shape sequence

A row of five boxes shows an arrow rotating 45° clockwise each time, while a small dot inside it moves from corner to corner. The sixth box is missing. The child should check one feature at a time: first the rotation (45° clockwise), then the dot (always in the top-right of the arrow's new position). Sequences reward this two-pass habit: find the rule for the main shape, then the rule for the detail. Most wrong answers come from applying only the first rule.

Worked example: a matrix puzzle

A 3×3 grid shows squares where the number of sides increases left to right (triangle, square, pentagon) and the shading alternates top to bottom (white, grey, striped). The missing cell needs a striped pentagon. Teach your child to read the grid both ways: across for shape, down for shading. If one rule gives two possible answers, the second rule settles it.

Worked example: a cube net

A flattened net shows three faces: a circle, a cross, and a triangle. Four 3D cubes are offered; only one can be folded from the net. The method is elimination: pick two faces that share an edge in the net, then reject every cube where those faces touch wrongly or appear opposite when they should be adjacent. Practising with a paper cut-out makes the folding real, not abstract.

How is non-verbal reasoning scored and why is it used in selection?

Raw marks are rarely used directly. Most 11+ papers, including GL Assessment ones, are turned into standardised scores. Marks are weighted for your child's age in months, so a child born late in the school year is not penalised against an older classmate. GL Assessment explains this age-weighting approach in its guidance on standardised scoring. Schools then set a pass mark or rank applicants by total score across papers.

Why include non-verbal reasoning at all? Grammar schools argue it measures pattern and spatial logic that can be worked out from a picture alone, so it depends less on vocabulary, reading age and heavy verbal tutoring. A child from a home with fewer books or less paid preparation is not automatically starting behind.

That is the rationale for the test, and no test is entirely tutor-proof. Children who have seen matrix and odd-one-out formats tend to feel more at ease than those meeting them cold, so short, regular practice before the exam is worth doing.

How can children practise non-verbal logical reasoning questions at home?

Short daily sessions beat long weekly ones. Ten to fifteen minutes a day of shape, pattern and spatial puzzles keeps the skills fresh without exhausting a nine-year-old after school. Mix paper and screen: GL-style papers teach timing and pencil discipline, while an app gives instant marking and a question bank big enough that your child cannot memorise the answers.

When should 11+ preparation for non-verbal reasoning start?

Start light in the summer term of Year 4: two or three short sessions a week, 10-15 minutes each, focused on spotting the main question types rather than finishing papers. Non-verbal reasoning is unfamiliar to most children, so early exposure removes the shock of seeing shape puzzles in a timed test.

Year 5 is the working year. From September, aim for four or five short sessions a week, then introduce timed sets and full papers in the spring, roughly 8-12 weeks before the exam. Consistency beats cramming: a daily 15 minutes builds pattern recognition far better than a two-hour Sunday session.

Watch for burnout signals, such as rushing answers or refusing to open the app. If practice becomes a battle, cut the session length, not the frequency, and keep one rest day a week.

FAQ

Is non-verbal reasoning the same as maths?

No. Maths tests taught curriculum content like number work and arithmetic, while non-verbal reasoning tests pattern and spatial logic using shapes alone. The two overlap only slightly: both reward careful, step-by-step thinking. A child who struggles with maths can still do well at NVR, and a strong mathematician may find rotation and code questions unfamiliar at first.

Can you improve non-verbal reasoning, or is it innate?

It improves with practice. The question types are a small, repeatable set, and each has a fixed solving routine a child can rehearse. Children who have seen matrix and odd-one-out formats score higher than those meeting them cold. Short daily sessions of 10-15 minutes build pattern recognition better than occasional long sessions.

Do all grammar schools include non-verbal reasoning in their 11+?

No. It depends on the school and the exam board the area uses. GL Assessment papers, used in Barnet, Sutton and other selective areas, typically include non-verbal reasoning alongside verbal reasoning, maths and English. Other areas structure their papers differently, so check your local council's admissions page to confirm what your target exam contains.

How many non-verbal reasoning questions are in a GL Assessment 11+ paper?

It varies by area and year, because schools commission different paper structures rather than one fixed format. Some areas test NVR as a separate short paper; others mix it into a longer paper. Check your local admissions page for the stated format, then match practice materials to that structure so timing drills reflect the real test.

What is verbal non-verbal reasoning in CEM-style papers?

CEM-style papers blend verbal and visual elements into mixed-format questions, so a single item may combine words, codes and shapes rather than sitting in a clearly labelled NVR section. Children cannot rely on question-type labels and must read each item carefully. Practising both question families separately still transfers well to this blended format.
